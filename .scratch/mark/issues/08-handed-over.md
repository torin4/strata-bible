# 08 — Movement 4, handed over

**What to build:** The passion, and the stop.

Movement 4 (`handed-over`, chapters 14–16), four readings, plus movement 3's capstone and doorway.

`mark-14a` is the woman with the jar, the supper, Gethsemane and the arrest. Mark's Gethsemane is
the least composed portrait of him in the four gospels, and the reading leaves it that way.

`mark-14b` is the trial and Peter in the courtyard, which is another sandwich: Mark cuts between
the man being condemned for saying who he is and the man outside swearing he does not know him.

`mark-15` is the sentence, the crucifixion, the cry from the cross and the centurion. The cry is
the hardest line in the book and it is not repaired here with a line from another gospel. The
centurion's sentence is the book's own answer to the question it has been asking since chapter one,
and Mark chose to put it in the mouth of the man who ran the execution.

`mark-16` is 16:1–8 and stops there. The women say nothing to anyone, because they were afraid, and
that is the last sentence of the book.

## Comments

**Gethsemane is left uncomposed, as the ticket asked.** He asks for the cup to be taken away, asks
three times, nothing answers, and he goes anyway, which the reading says plainly is a different
thing from not minding. Mark's verb for his distress is strong enough that later writers softened
it, and the ground note says so rather than the reading arguing about it.

**The young man in the linen cloth is left unexplained**, because Mark leaves him unexplained. Two
verses, no name, in no other gospel, placed at the exact moment the book empties of everybody. The
reading notes that Mark thought it worth the space and does not speculate further.

**The cry from the cross carries the movement's misreading**, and it is the reading most likely to
be objected to. The Psalm 22 defence is named as a real reading with a long history, and then set
against what Mark actually did: one line, in Aramaic, which he reserves for words he wants heard as
themselves; no quotation of the psalm's ending; bystanders who mishear it as a call for Elijah; and
Luke and John both replacing the line entirely. Mark's Jesus dies asking why, and the reading says
so without deciding what that means about him.

**The two torn things are the frame.** Mark uses the tearing verb exactly twice, at the heavens in
chapter 1 and at the curtain in chapter 15, and the reading in 1:9-13 planted it fourteen chapters
before this one collects it. At the first nobody in the story hears the voice. At the second a
Roman officer says the sentence, having watched him die, and is the only human in the book to say
it and not be silenced.

**16:8 ends where the manuscripts end.** No gesture past it. The reading notes that the Greek stops
on a conjunction, that this is why the lost-ending argument exists, and that the text as read for
two thousand years names the failure of everybody, including the last three people left, and then
hands the telling to whoever is holding it.
