# 07 — Movement 3, the temple

**What to build:** Six days in Jerusalem, and the chapter everyone misuses.

Movement 3 (`the-temple`, chapters 11–13), three readings, plus movement 2's capstone and doorway.

`mark-11` is the entry, the fig tree and the temple. The fig tree is wrapped around the temple
action on purpose, which is Mark's sandwiching technique, and the reading names it: the tree and
the temple are being said about each other.

`mark-12` is the parable of the tenants, the questions designed as traps, and the widow putting in
two small coins. The widow is usually preached as a model of generosity. Read in sequence, directly
after he has condemned scribes who devour widows' houses, it is at least as likely to be a lament
about what the temple takes from the poor. The reading gives both and does not settle it.

`mark-13` is the discourse. Its misreading is load-bearing: this is not a timetable. It is a
first-century text about the destruction of a specific building, written near or just after that
building came down, and reading it as a calendar has done real damage.

## Comments

**The fig tree and the temple are read as one act**, and the detail that makes it work is the one
most retellings would remove: it was not the season for figs. Mark put that in. A version where the
tree is at fault would work better and he declined to write it. The misreading names the temper
reading and refuses it, and also refuses the other easy one, that the temple action is a protest
against commerce. The trade was the mechanism the law required. The quotation he chose is about a
building used as a safe house by people whose conduct happens elsewhere.

**The widow gets both readings and no verdict**, exactly as the ticket asked. She is a model of
giving, and she is also what the sentence immediately before her describes, a widow's house being
devoured, putting her last two coins into a building he is about to say will be pulled down. Mark
sets them side by side and moves into chapter 13, and so does the reading.

**The tenants parable is handled where it has done the most damage.** The vineyard given to others
has been read for centuries as a transfer from one people to another. Inside its own scene the
tenants are a named leadership in one city in one week. The vineyard is not taken from Israel; it
is taken from the men running it. Said once, plainly, and not laboured.

**Chapter 13's misreading is the load-bearing one in the movement.** Almost everything in it is
local and dated: flee Judea, do not go back for your coat, hope it is not winter. The cosmic
imagery is standard prophetic vocabulary for a political order falling, used of Babylon and Egypt.
And the chapter refuses to give a day, in its own words. The reading says the cost of the calendar
reading has never been theoretical, and leaves it there.

**The tension is internal to the chapter**, which is a first for this book: all these things within
this generation, and four verses later nobody knows the day, not the angels and not the Son. The
same speech, unreconciled. Copyists were uncomfortable enough that some manuscripts of Matthew drop
the phrase about the Son.
